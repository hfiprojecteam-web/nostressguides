Pass & Transport Mistakes
6 mistakes
Swiss Travel Pass — wrong buyer profile
Buying the Swiss Travel Pass for a city-based trip
The Swiss Travel Pass makes financial sense only when your total train travel exceeds its cost. A 4-day trip based entirely in Zurich with one day trip to Lucerne involves perhaps CHF 132 in train tickets. The 4-day pass costs CHF 309. Individual tickets win by CHF 177. The pass is designed for multi-region travelers covering significant distances daily. Before buying, add up your planned routes on sbb.ch. If the total falls below the pass price, buy point-to-point tickets.
Who the pass is actually forTravelers covering multiple regions over several days, visiting mountain excursions (the 50% discount compounds fast), and anyone wanting museum access included. Single-city visitors and those renting a car for most legs: buy individual tickets or the Half Fare Card instead.
Swiss Travel Pass — activation
Forgetting to activate the pass before boarding
The Swiss Travel Pass requires activation before first use. An unactivated pass is invalid. Activate at a staffed SBB ticket counter or through the SBB Mobile app. Swiss train conductors check passes on board — an unactivated pass produces the same result as having no ticket. The fine is the full point-to-point fare plus a processing fee. This happens more often than it should.
Swiss trains — punctuality
Arriving at the platform after the departure second
Swiss trains depart on the scheduled second. Not approximately. Not when the platform is full. At the scheduled second. Being 30 seconds late on the platform means watching your train leave. The Swiss system is built on precise connections — a train waited for any passenger becomes a cascade of delays across the network. Arrive at the platform at least 3 minutes before departure. Check the platform number in the SBB app or on station displays (platforms change occasionally).
Mountain excursions — pricing
Paying full price for mountain railways without checking discounts
The Swiss Travel Pass provides 50% off most major mountain railways (Jungfraujoch, Gornergrat, Schilthorn, Pilatus, Titlis). The Half Fare Card provides the same 50%. Rigi, Stoos, Stanserhorn, Klewenalp, and Brunni are now free with the Swiss Travel Pass. The discount is not applied automatically at ticket machines — you must select the appropriate reduction option or show your pass at the counter. On Jungfraujoch, the difference is CHF 261.20 vs. CHF 177.20. On Gornergrat: CHF 132 vs. CHF 66. Not asking is an expensive oversight.
Always ask before purchasingAt every mountain railway ticket window: “Do you have a discount for Swiss Travel Pass holders?” or “Rabatt mit Swiss Travel Pass?” The answer is almost always yes, but the discount is not applied unless you specifically request or select it.
Supersaver tickets — missed savings
Ignoring Supersaver tickets for fixed-itinerary travelers
If your train routes are decided in advance, Supersaver tickets on sbb.ch offer 30–50% off standard fares. A Zurich–Interlaken ticket at full price is CHF 76; a Supersaver on the same route can be CHF 38–52. Non-refundable and non-changeable, but for anyone with a fixed itinerary these represent the single biggest saving available on Swiss rail. Available on sbb.ch and the SBB Mobile app. Limited quantities per train — book as soon as your dates are confirmed.
Zermatt — driving
Attempting to drive to Zermatt
Zermatt is entirely car-free. No road reaches the village. This is not a preference or a seasonal restriction — it is the permanent physical reality. The road ends at Täsch, 5 km before Zermatt. From there, only the shuttle train reaches the village. Park at Täsch (CHF 11–16 per day) and take the 12-minute train (CHF 17.20 round trip). Factor parking and shuttle costs into your Zermatt budget. The same applies to Wengen, Mürren, and Gimmelwald in the Bernese Oberland.
Scenic Train Traps
2 to understand
Glacier Express — Zermatt to St. Moritz (or reverse)
The Glacier Express supplement for views you can see for free
The Glacier Express is a beautifully operated train and a genuine experience. It is also poor value if you are paying only for the scenery. The mandatory seat reservation is a flat CHF 54 (1st and 2nd class, year-round as of 2026 — the fee no longer varies by season) on top of your ticket or pass. Optional onboard lunch runs CHF 36 (dish of the day) to CHF 49 (3-course menu of the day). Total cost on top of a Swiss Travel Pass: roughly CHF 90–103 for a single journey.

The alternative: regular regional trains cover the identical route on the same tracks. You change at Andermatt and Disentis. The journey takes roughly the same time. The views are identical. Windows on regional trains open for unobstructed photography. The Swiss Travel Pass covers everything at no extra cost. No supplement, no lunch, no reservation required.

Take the Glacier Express if you want the curated experience — table service, large panoramic windows, a formal journey. Skip it if the supplement and lunch budget would fund another mountain excursion you actually want.
The fog problemThe Glacier Express takes 8 hours. Mountain weather is unpredictable. Paying CHF 90–103 in supplement and lunch for 8 hours of cloud cover is a real possibility in any season. Check weather forecasts for the Oberalp Pass corridor before committing. No weather refund exists.
Bernina Express — Chur to Tirano
The Bernina Express supplement when regular trains use the same tracks
The Bernina Line is genuinely exceptional — UNESCO World Heritage, the highest transalpine railway without rack assistance, palm trees in Tirano after departing the glacier. The mandatory seat reservation for the full Chur–Tirano route is CHF 40 (low season) to CHF 44 (high season, 2 May–25 October) — up sharply from the CHF 14–26 reported in earlier editions. The shorter St. Moritz–Tirano segment is CHF 32 flat.

Identical logic to the Glacier Express applies: regional trains run the same route on the same tracks at no supplement. The Bernina Express panoramic cars have larger windows; regular trains have opening windows. Both see the same viaducts, same glacier, same descent into Italy. The supplement buys the panoramic car and the formal reservation, not exclusive access to the scenery.

The price gap with the Glacier Express (flat CHF 54) has narrowed considerably. If you are traveling on a tight budget, the free regional-train alternative still exists on both routes.
Exception to noteThe GoldenPass Express (Interlaken to Montreux) requires only a CHF 19 reservation — no supplement on top. The base fare is covered by the Swiss Travel Pass. This is the most practical scenic route for pass holders and the one we consistently recommend.
Money & Currency Traps
3 active
Card terminals — everywhere in Switzerland
Dynamic Currency Conversion — always decline
When paying by card, terminals regularly ask whether you want to pay in your home currency (USD, EUR, GBP) rather than Swiss francs. This screen is designed to look helpful — it shows you an amount in a familiar currency. Always decline. Always select CHF. The conversion rate offered by the merchant includes a markup of 3–7% above the real rate. Your own bank charges a foreign transaction fee of 1–3% but applies the real rate. The merchant’s rate is always worse. This applies to hotels, restaurants, shops, and mountain railways. Every time, without exception.
Airports — all three Swiss airports
Airport currency exchange counters
Currency exchange counters at Zurich, Geneva, and Basel airports apply rates 5–10% worse than ATM rates. There is no advantage to using them. Withdraw Swiss francs from a Bancomat or Bankomat ATM (attached to actual Swiss banks: UBS, Credit Suisse, PostFinance, Raiffeisen). Your home bank charges 1–3% in foreign transaction fees but at the real exchange rate — significantly better than any counter rate. Withdraw CHF 200–300 at a time to minimize per-transaction fees. Do not withdraw more than you need — reconverting Swiss francs back to your home currency at departure means paying the spread twice.
Restaurants accepting euros — tourist zones
Paying in euros where CHF is the actual currency
Some restaurants in tourist areas and border towns accept euros. This sounds convenient. The exchange rate applied is typically 5–8% worse than the real rate. Change is given in Swiss francs at their rate. If you pay a CHF 60 meal in euros, you effectively overpay by CHF 3–5. Pay in Swiss francs with your card, or withdraw CHF from an ATM before dining. The moment a restaurant displays a euro price alongside a franc price, compare both and pay in CHF.
Tourist Traps by Region
6 zones
Lucerne
Lucerne — Schweizerhofquai and Nationalquai waterfront
Lakefront restaurants along the main promenade
The waterfront promenade facing the lake and the KKL concert hall is Lucerne’s most photographed stretch. The restaurants lining Schweizerhofquai have excellent views and price accordingly. The food is overpriced and designed for tourists who will not return. A meal here costs 40–60% more than equivalent food five minutes’ walk into the old town’s interior streets. The promenade is worth walking. The restaurants are not worth sitting in.
Where to eat insteadWalk off the main waterfront into the old town interior. Rathaus Brauerei at Unter der Egg: house-brewed beer and solid traditional plates. Wirtshaus Galliker (south of old town): Lucerne’s most authentic traditional cooking, primarily local crowd. CHF 35–55 for dinner.
Interlaken
Interlaken — Höheweg main street
Restaurants on the main tourist boulevard
Interlaken functions primarily as a transit hub for the Bernese Oberland. Its main street (Höheweg) between the two train stations concentrates hotels, souvenir shops, and restaurants aimed entirely at tourist throughput. Fondue for CHF 45 in a room of other tourists is not the Swiss fondue experience. The better option: if you want fondue, eat it in Grindelwald, Wengen, or Mürren in a mountain setting. In Interlaken itself, find the residential streets behind Höheweg for local pricing.
Jungfraujoch
Jungfraujoch — 3,454m above sea level
Going to Jungfraujoch on a bad weather day
Jungfraujoch costs CHF 261.20 at full price, CHF 177.20 with Swiss Travel Pass (high season), plus the mandatory CHF 10 seat reservation. The entire value of the excursion depends on clear weather. On a cloudy day at the summit, you are paying CHF 187–271 to stand inside a fog bank. The summit is inside the mountain for much of the visit (Ice Palace, tunnels), but the famous views of the Aletsch Glacier require clear conditions. Check the Jungfraujoch webcam at jungfrau.ch the night before and at 07:00 the day of. If the Sphinx Observatory is hidden, postpone. The mandatory reservation system means you must either buy Flex Cancellation at purchase, or accept the risk.
Zermatt
Zermatt — main street restaurant zone
Restaurants on Bahnhofstrasse facing the Matterhorn-view terraces
Zermatt is one of Switzerland’s most expensive resorts. The restaurants on the main pedestrian street with Matterhorn-view terraces charge a significant premium for that view. A standard fondue that costs CHF 25–30 elsewhere reaches CHF 40–50 here. The Matterhorn appears above any restaurant in Zermatt whenever the sky is clear — the view premium buys nothing you cannot get by walking to a free viewpoint. Walk one street back from Bahnhofstrasse for prices that reflect the food rather than the real estate.
Budget-friendly alternative baseSleeping in Täsch (5km outside Zermatt) cuts accommodation costs 30–40%. The 12-minute shuttle train runs regularly. The food in Täsch costs what food costs in a normal Swiss village.
Zürich & Geneva
Zürich — Limmat riverfront · Geneva — Quai du Mont-Blanc
Lakefront and riverfront restaurant zones in both cities
Both Zürich and Geneva have well-documented tourist-restaurant zones along their waterfronts. In Zürich, the Limmat riverfront between the main station and the old town concentrates overpriced cafés that trade on proximity to the Grossmünster and Fraumünster. In Geneva, Quai du Mont-Blanc facing the Jet d’Eau applies lake-view pricing to average food. Both cities have active local dining scenes one to two streets removed from the tourist axis. Walk until you stop seeing menus in four languages displayed outside.
Nationwide
Switzerland-wide — mountain summit restaurants
Eating at summit restaurants as a meal strategy
Every major Swiss summit has a restaurant. All charge altitude premiums: CHF 25–40 for a basic plate, CHF 6–9 for coffee, CHF 10–14 for a beer. These prices exist because there is no competition and everything must be flown in by helicopter or carried up by cogwheel train. This is not a scam — the logistics are real. But treating the summit restaurant as a dining option rather than an emergency stop is expensive. The strategy: eat a proper meal in the valley before ascending, bring snacks, and treat any summit food as a deliberate splurge on atmosphere rather than nutrition.
ExceptionThe Migros and Coop supermarkets in every Swiss train station sell excellent ready-made sandwiches, salads, and snacks at normal prices. A picnic assembled at the base station before the ascent costs CHF 8–15 for what the summit restaurant charges CHF 35–50 for.
Classic First-Timer Mistakes
6 mistakes
Car rental
Renting a car “for flexibility” and not using it
Switzerland’s public transport reaches every tourist destination a visitor is likely to want. Trains, PostBuses, lake boats, and cable cars operate on coordinated timetables that connect seamlessly. Most first-time visitors who rent a car “for flexibility” discover that the car creates complications: parking in cities costs CHF 20–40 per day, the motorway vignette is CHF 40, fuel runs CHF 1.85 per liter, and several key destinations (Zermatt, Wengen, Mürren, Gimmelwald) are car-free. The train is faster than driving between major cities and requires no navigation, parking, or fuel. Rent a car only if your itinerary specifically requires it: remote valleys, cross-country combining Switzerland with France or Italy, or traveling with young children and heavy luggage.
Motorway
Driving on Swiss motorways without the vignette
The annual motorway vignette (CHF 40) is mandatory for driving on Swiss motorways and expressways. It is valid from December 1 of the previous year through January 31 of the following year — essentially a full year. There is no pro-rated daily or weekly option. Fines for driving without a vignette run CHF 100–200 plus the cost of the vignette. Buy at the border crossing when entering Switzerland, at petrol stations, or online before arrival. If you rented a car at a Swiss airport, the vignette is typically already on the car — confirm with the rental company before driving.
Mountain excursions
Planning mountain excursions in April–May or October–November
Most cable cars, gondolas, and cogwheel trains outside major year-round operations close for annual maintenance in these shoulder windows. The Pilatus cogwheel railway (the world’s steepest) closes mid-November through mid-May. Other cable cars have similar windows. Arriving at a closed lift station wastes the travel time and the entry to the valley. Before finalizing any itinerary involving a specific mountain excursion, check the operating dates on that mountain railway’s website. This applies to every non-famous mountain — the Jungfrau railways and Gornergrat operate year-round; most others do not.
Budgeting
Budgeting in dollars or euros instead of Swiss francs
Switzerland prices everything in CHF. A cappuccino at CHF 6.50 looks alarming in dollars. The Jungfraujoch at CHF 261.20 sounds catastrophic in any currency. But the practical question is always: how many CHF do I have, and how much does this cost in CHF? Converting on the fly adds confusion and causes people to either over-budget (and deny themselves things they can actually afford) or under-budget (by mentally discounting the exchange rate). Set a daily CHF budget. Withdraw CHF from ATMs. Think in CHF throughout the trip. As of August 2026, CHF 1 ≈ USD 1.23 — the franc has strengthened notably since early 2026, so re-check the current rate rather than assuming rough parity.
Mountain weather
Underestimating how quickly Alpine weather changes
A sunny morning at 2,500 meters routinely becomes a thunderstorm by early afternoon. This is not a rare event — it is the normal Alpine weather pattern from June through August. Hikers wearing t-shirts at 9:00 AM may encounter hail by 14:00 at the same altitude. Always carry a waterproof layer regardless of morning conditions. Check the MeteoSwiss forecast (app available, in English) before any mountain excursion. The Swiss Alpine Club (SAC) huts provide emergency shelter on major routes. Tell someone your hiking plan. Turn back if conditions deteriorate. People fall and get lost on well-maintained Swiss trails every season.
Cash
Relying entirely on cards above the treeline
Cards work in Swiss cities, train stations, hotels, and most restaurants without exception. Above the treeline, the picture changes. Mountain huts, small cable car operators, and remote trail-side refreshment stands frequently lack card terminals or operate them unreliably. Carry CHF 50–100 in cash for any day that involves mountain huts, hiking above 1,800m, or remote villages. The same applies to market vendors and small bakeries in smaller towns throughout Switzerland.
