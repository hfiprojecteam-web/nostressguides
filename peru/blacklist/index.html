20 things to avoid
Altitude • Transport • Tourist Traps • Booking Mistakes
Peru is extraordinary — one of the most culturally rich and geographically dramatic countries on Earth. The traps on this list are not Peruvian. They are the predictable patterns that grow wherever iconic destinations attract mass tourism. Knowing them in advance means your experience — and your money — goes where it should.
How to use this list These are patterns, not universal rules. Every item describes a consistent, repeatable situation — not an indictment of a region or profession. Use this list to recognise the moment it’s happening, so you can make an informed choice.

Health & Altitude
5 patterns
The most predictable mistake in Peru
Cusco — 3,400 m — first 24 hours
Ignoring altitude sickness on arrival day
Soroche (altitude sickness) affects almost everyone arriving in Cusco from sea level. The symptoms — headache, nausea, breathlessness — can hit within 2 hours. The mistake is treating the first day normally: walking fast, eating heavily, drinking alcohol. That night you will be face-down. Every year thousands of visitors ruin their first Cusco days by not resting.
The ruleArrive in Cusco and stop. Rest the whole day. No alcohol for 24-48 hours. Eat light. Drink coca tea — it genuinely helps. If you feel fine by evening, you can walk slowly around the Plaza de Armas. Diamox (acetazolamide) helps some people — consult a doctor before travel.
Rainbow Mountain — Vinicunca — 5,200 m
Attempting Rainbow Mountain without acclimatization
The mountain has become so famous that visitors sometimes book the day trip on their first or second day in Cusco. At 5,200 m — higher than any mountain in the Alps or Rockies — this is a serious medical risk. People have died on this trail from altitude-related emergencies. It is not a dramatic hike if you are acclimatized. It is potentially dangerous if you are not.
The ruleMinimum 2-3 full days acclimatizing in Cusco (3,400 m) before attempting Rainbow Mountain. If you struggle walking uphill in Cusco after 2 days, take one more day. Horses are available at the trailhead for S/40 — use them without shame if you are struggling.
Puno — Lake Titicaca — 3,810 m
Underestimating altitude at Puno after Arequipa
Arequipa at 2,335 m feels fine. Cusco at 3,400 m feels manageable after a rest day. Puno at 3,810 m is higher than both — and many travellers who felt acclimatized in Cusco are surprised to feel bad again in Puno. Adding the Colca Canyon rim (4,800 m) between Arequipa and Puno compounds the exposure.
The ruleTreat Puno as a new acclimatization challenge even if Cusco felt fine. Rest the first afternoon. Do not schedule the lake tour on arrival day. The Titicaca crossing to Bolivia adds another few hours at extreme altitude — hydrate aggressively.
Lima — pharmacies citywide
Buying Diamox in Peru expecting it to be easy
Acetazolamide (Diamox) requires a prescription in Peru and is not always stocked. The trap: assuming you can buy altitude medication on arrival in Lima before flying to Cusco. Some pharmacies have it; many do not. You end up spending 3 hours in a taxi hitting pharmacies instead of resting.
The ruleGet a prescription from your doctor at home before travelling. Bring a full course. Generic acetazolamide is available in the UK, USA, and most EU countries without difficulty if you plan ahead.
Colca Canyon — Arequipa region
Descending to the canyon floor without guide or preparation
The trek to the Colca Canyon floor and back is a 2-day serious physical undertaking at high altitude. Most visitors do the rim drive, which is entirely different. The mistake is treating "going into the canyon" as a casual half-day activity. The descent is steep, the altitude fluctuates, and the climb back out can take 4-6 hours. People have needed rescue.
The ruleIf you plan to trek the canyon floor: hire a local guide, start before 06:00, carry 3+ litres of water, and do not do it after the rain season (Jan-Apr when trails wash out). The rim viewpoints are genuinely spectacular without descending at all.
Transport Scams
5 patterns
Lima, Cusco — the taxi trap
Lima — Jorge Chávez Airport
Taking unregistered taxis from the airport
Men approach you in the arrivals hall offering “taxi, very cheap.” These are unregistered, uninsured, and in some cases used for express kidnapping — where you are driven to an ATM and forced to withdraw cash. This is not an exaggeration. It happens regularly to tourists at Lima airport. The price they offer is not cheap compared to the legitimate alternatives.
The ruleUse Uber (works well in Lima), InDriver, or the official green-uniformed taxi booth inside the arrivals hall. Never get into a vehicle with someone who approached you unsolicited. The official green taxi to Miraflores costs around S/65-80 — agree the price before getting in.
Cusco — city taxis
Hailing street taxis in Cusco without agreeing a price first
Cusco taxis have no meters. The standard fare for any journey within the city is S/5-8. Tourist prices begin at S/15-25 and climb from there if you look uncertain. The trap: getting in and then negotiating — you have lost all leverage at that point. The driver knows you are committed.
The ruleAgree the fare before you open the door. Say the destination, state the price you expect (S/5-8 within centre), and wait for agreement. If they say S/20, walk to the next taxi. Uber works in Cusco and is fairer. For airport transfers, book through your hotel.
Cusco — San Pedro station area
Buying Machu Picchu train tickets from touts near the station
Men near San Pedro station approach travellers saying they have “last-minute train tickets” for Aguas Calientes. These are either counterfeit, cancelled, or sold at 3x the face value. PeruRail and Inca Rail both sell only through their official websites and ticketing offices. There is no legitimate secondary market.
The ruleBook at perurail.com or incarail.com only. Book months in advance — trains genuinely sell out. If trains are sold out: try Ollantaytambo-only legs (shorter, sometimes available last-minute), or consider the 4-day Inca Trail trek as an alternative that ends at Machu Picchu.
Peru — long-distance buses
Taking budget bus companies for overnight routes
Peru has excellent premium bus companies (Cruz del Sur, Oltursa, Movil Tours) and terrible cheap ones. On mountain routes, the difference is not just comfort — it is safety. Peru’s most dangerous roads are in the Andes, and accident rates on cheap lines are significantly higher. The cheapest option on a mountain overnight route is not a bargain.
The ruleFor any Andean overnight route (Arequipa-Puno, Nazca-Arequipa, Puno-Cusco, Lima-Nazca), use Cruz del Sur Cama or Oltursa Cama only. The difference is S/30-50, the difference in safety is not comparable. For coastal Pan-American routes, second-tier companies are acceptable.
Lima — airport currency exchange
Exchanging money at the airport on arrival
The exchange desks at Jorge Chávez Airport offer rates 8-12% below the street rate in Miraflores. On $500, that is a $50 difference. The trap: taking out a large amount “just to be safe” at the airport booth when you arrive. You lose significantly for the convenience of not waiting until tomorrow.
The ruleExchange only a small amount at the airport if you need immediate cash (S/50-100 for taxis). Get the rest from Banco de Crédito ATMs in Miraflores, or from the licensed exchange bureaux (casa de cambio) in Miraflores — rates are consistently better. Never exchange with street money changers.
Tourist Traps
5 patterns
Machu Picchu — Cusco — Lima
Machu Picchu — entry tickets
Assuming you can buy Machu Picchu tickets on arrival
Since 2023, Machu Picchu operates on timed entry circuits with a daily visitor cap. The popular circuits (1, 2, and 4 for the Sun Gate) sell out weeks to months in advance, especially in high season (May-September). The trap: arriving in Aguas Calientes and discovering that all tickets for the next 3 days are sold out. This happens to hundreds of visitors every year.
The ruleBook at machupicchu.gob.pe at least 2-4 months in advance in high season. Buy train AND entry separately — they are different systems. If last-minute: Circuit 3 (the mountain viewpoints only) often has availability when Circuits 1 and 2 are sold out. Huayna Picchu and Machu Picchu Mountain require additional tickets booked far in advance.
Aguas Calientes — hotels and restaurants
The Aguas Calientes tourist price bubble
Aguas Calientes (the town at the base of Machu Picchu) has no road access, which means every product is brought in by train. This creates a captive market — food, accommodation, and souvenirs are typically 2-3x the price of Cusco or Ollantaytambo. A basic hotel room that costs S/80 in Ollantaytambo costs S/200-300 in Aguas Calientes.
The ruleBase yourself in Ollantaytambo the night before and take the first morning train. This saves S/100-200 per night and gives you a more authentic experience. If you must stay in Aguas Calientes, Indio Feliz restaurant offers the best value and quality — avoid the tourist strip along the river.
Cusco — Pisac market — everywhere
Buying alpaca products without knowing what real alpaca looks like
Peru is famous for alpaca and vicuna wool. The markets are full of “100% baby alpaca” items that are actually acrylic or a very low percentage blend. At S/30 for a “baby alpaca” scarf, you are almost certainly buying synthetic. Real baby alpaca feels exceptionally soft, costs substantially more, and has no static. The price difference between real and fake is 5-10x.
The ruleBuy alpaca from established weavers’ cooperatives (Chinchero has genuine ones), or from reputable Cusco shops like Centro de Textiles Tradicionales del Cusco. A genuine baby alpaca sweater costs S/250-500+. If it costs S/60, it is not baby alpaca. Burn a strand — real alpaca smells like hair, synthetic smells like plastic.
Lima — Miraflores tour desks
Booking all tours through hotel tour desks
Hotel tour desks in Lima and Cusco typically operate as intermediaries and add 20-40% margin to tour prices. The exact same Nazca overflight, Ballestas Islands tour, or Colca Canyon trip can be booked directly with the operator for significantly less. The Ballestas Islands boat sold for S/80 at the hotel tour desk costs S/40 at the pier.
The ruleFor any activity, compare hotel desk price with the price of walking to the operator directly. Paracas pier (Ballestas boats), Nazca airport (overflight operators), and Arequipa agency street (Colca tours) all have competitive direct prices. For complex itineraries, a reputable Peruvian agency (not a hotel desk) is more efficient than individual direct booking.
Uros floating islands — Lake Titicaca
The commercialised Uros experience
The Uros floating reed islands are one of Peru’s most photographed attractions. They are also intensely commercialised — several islands are essentially outdoor markets where community members perform traditional activities for cameras while selling aggressively. The entry price of S/10 does not include the purchase pressure you will feel on the island. Budget S/80-150 in unofficial “gifts” and purchases if you want to leave gracefully.
The ruleThe Uros is worth visiting with calibrated expectations: it is photogenic, culturally interesting despite the commercialisation, and short (2h). For a more authentic experience, continue to Taquile Island (3h further) where the community genuinely preserves weaving traditions for their own cultural continuity, not for tourism performance.
Planning & Booking Mistakes
5 patterns
Before you arrive — the booking window matters enormously in Peru
Nazca overflight — Nazca, Ica region
Eating before a Nazca Lines overflight
The Nazca overflight is done in small Cessna aircraft (5-8 passengers) that bank steeply to show all the figures. The manoeuvres are aggressive and the cabin is hot. Motion sickness affects approximately 30-40% of passengers, even people who never get motion sick. A full breakfast immediately before is the most reliable way to ensure a miserable 30 minutes.
The ruleEat nothing for 2 hours before the flight. Book the earliest morning slot (07:00-08:00) when the air is calmest. Take an anti-nausea tablet (Dramamine/Biodramina) 1 hour before if you are prone to motion sickness. Sit in the front row of the aircraft if offered a choice — smoother.
Peru — Inca Trail — bookings
Trying to book the Inca Trail less than 6 months in advance
The classic 4-day Inca Trail trek is limited to 500 permits per day (including guides and porters), which means approximately 200 trekkers. Permits sell out months in advance for the May-September high season. The mistake: deciding you want to do the Inca Trail during your trip, or booking 8 weeks out in high season. You will find it sold out entirely.
The ruleBook the Inca Trail 6-12 months in advance for May-September. Alternatives if sold out: the Salkantay Trek (equally spectacular, no permits required, 5 days), Lares Trek (more cultural, passes through Andean villages), or Inca Jungle Trek (bike + hike combo). All end at Machu Picchu by train from Aguas Calientes.
Peru — general planning
Underestimating distances and overloading the itinerary
Peru looks manageable on a map until you look at the actual travel times. Lima to Cusco is 1.5h by plane but 24h by bus. Cusco to Puno is 6h. Arequipa to Colca Canyon is 3h. The classic mistake: planning to see Lima, Paracas, Nazca, Arequipa, Colca, Puno, Cusco, Sacred Valley, Machu Picchu, and Rainbow Mountain in 10 days. This is physically possible and experientially disastrous.
The ruleChoose a circuit and commit to it: either the north-south loop (Lima→Paracas→Nazca→Arequipa→Puno→Cusco) or the highlands focus (Lima→Cusco→Sacred Valley→Machu Picchu→Rainbow Mountain). Allow at least 2 full days in Cusco before any highland activity. Quality over quantity.
Peru — rainy season planning
Visiting Rainbow Mountain or the Inca Trail in January–March
The Peruvian rainy season runs November-April, peaking in January-March. In the highlands, this means muddy trails, low cloud obscuring views, and in some cases, complete closures. Rainbow Mountain is impassable under snow from January to March. The Inca Trail closes entirely every February for maintenance. The famous “rainbow” colours of Vinicunca are only visible when dry.
The ruleFor highland activities (Rainbow Mountain, Inca Trail, Colca Canyon, Sacred Valley), plan for May-October. November and April are shoulder season — possible but unpredictable. Lima and Paracas are fine year-round. The Amazon is actually more spectacular in high-water season (Dec-May) if jungle is your focus.
Peru — cash and payments
Relying on cards outside Lima and Cusco city centres
Peru runs primarily on cash outside major cities. Aguas Calientes has 2 ATMs that frequently run out of money and charge high fees. The Sacred Valley has almost no ATMs. Paracas, Colca Canyon area, and smaller towns operate cash-only almost entirely. The trap: arriving somewhere remote with no soles and expecting to pay by card.
The ruleWithdraw enough soles in Lima or Cusco (Banco de Crédito or Scotiabank for best rates) before heading anywhere remote. Aguas Calientes: withdraw in Cusco before the train. Colca Canyon: withdraw in Arequipa. Uros Islands: bring cash for everything — zero card infrastructure. Budget S/50-100/day extra for emergencies.
