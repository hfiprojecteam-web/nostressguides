Restaurants to Avoid
5 patterns
Brussels · Belgium-wide
Any restaurant directly on Grand Place or the Grote Markt
The restaurants with terraces directly facing Brussels’ Grand Place charge two to three times what an equivalent meal costs two streets away, for food that does not justify the premium. The same rule applies to Bruges’ Markt, Ghent’s Korenmarkt, and Antwerp’s Grote Markt. The principle is consistent across every Belgian city: proximity to the main square correlates directly with overpriced mediocrity. The view from the terrace is real. The cooking is not. Walk to a side street in any direction and the situation immediately improves.
Where to go instead in BrusselsRue des Bouchers is a tourist street but with better standards than Grand Place. Better: Saint-Gilles neighbourhood, Ixelles around Place Flagey, Matongé. In Bruges: Sint-Amandsstraat, Langestraat. In Ghent: Patershol, side streets off Vrijdagmarkt.
Bruges · Belgium-wide
Photo menus displayed outside · touts at the door
Any Belgian restaurant displaying photographs of dishes on an outdoor menu board is optimised for tourist throughput, not cooking. Any establishment with staff positioned outside to attract passing pedestrians has already answered the question of whether it needs to. Good Belgian restaurants fill by reputation. In Bruges especially, the correlation between outdoor photo menus and poor food-to-price ratio is near-total.
Brussels — near Manneken Pis and Grand Sablon
Tourist-menu restaurants in the Manneken Pis corridor
The streets immediately surrounding the Manneken Pis statue, running from Grand Place down Rue de l’Étuve, concentrate the densest cluster of tourist-menu restaurants in Belgium. Fixed-price menus at €25–35 for moules-frites and a beer that would cost €14–18 in any neighbourhood brasserie. The Grand Sablon area nearby is also tourist-priced, though with higher quality (and higher prices still). Walk to Saint-Gilles or the area around Place du Jeu de Balle for food that Belgians actually eat.
Belgium-wide — the waffle question
Tourist waffle stands in any Belgian city centre
There are two Belgian waffles: the Brussels waffle (rectangular, light, eaten with toppings) and the Liège waffle (round, dense, caramelised sugar baked in). Tourist-zone stands serve neither correctly. They sell large, rubbery Brussels-style waffles loaded with strawberries, Nutella, and whipped cream — a combination that no Belgian eats and that costs €8–12. A proper Liège waffle from a local bakery costs €1.50–2.50 and is eaten plain, warm, standing up. In Brussels, the best waffle is from a friterie or bakery, not a tourist stand in the shadow of the Atomium.
Where to find the real thingAny neighbourhood boulangerie or friterie outside the tourist core. In Brussels: Maison Dandoy (Brussels waffle institution, two locations). In Liège: any street stand in the city centre — the Liège waffle was born here.
Belgium-wide — chocolate shops
Belgian chocolate from tourist-route shops
Belgian chocolate is genuinely world-class. The tourist-route shops near Grand Place and in Bruges’ main shopping streets sell chocolate that ranges from adequate to mediocre at premium prices. The packaging is beautiful; the contents are often industrial. Seek artisan chocolatiers: in Brussels, Pierre Marcolini, Laurent Gerbaud, and Wittamer are the benchmarks. In Bruges, The Chocolate Line and Dumon. In Ghent, Yuzu. These cost more per piece but are incomparably better and are what you are actually paying Belgium’s reputation for.
Transport Traps
5 traps
Brussels Airport — arrivals hall
Unlicensed taxi drivers inside the terminal
Men with signs or approaching travelers inside the Brussels Airport arrivals hall are unlicensed and not regulated. They routinely charge €80–120 for the Brussels city centre journey that the regulated flat-rate taxi covers for €55. They are well-dressed and confident — this is not an obvious scam. Proceed directly to Level -1 of the terminal to the official taxi rank. Only use vehicles with the blue-and-yellow “Brussels Airport Taxi” livery. Alternatively, take the SNCB Airport Express train (every 20 minutes, €14.90 including Diabolo supplement, 17 minutes to Brussels-Central).
The simple ruleNever accept a ride offered to you inside an airport building. The legitimate taxi rank is always outside or on a dedicated level. This applies at Brussels Airport and at any airport in the world.
All airports and stations · tourist zones
Currency exchange counters
Currency exchange offices at Brussels Airport, Brussels-Midi station, and tourist areas offer rates 5–10% worse than ATM rates. Never use them. Use the ATM attached to a Belgian bank (BNP Paribas Fortis, KBC, ING, Belfius) instead. Withdraw €100–200 at a time to minimize per-transaction fees. If you arrive with no euros at all, an airport ATM is acceptable; the exchange counter next to it is not.
Card terminals — everywhere in Belgium
Dynamic Currency Conversion — always decline
When paying by card, terminals regularly offer to charge you in dollars or pounds instead of euros. Always select euros. The rate offered by the merchant includes a markup of 3–7% above your bank’s rate. Your bank’s foreign transaction fee (typically 1–3%) at the real rate is still cheaper than Dynamic Currency Conversion. This appears at hotels, restaurants, shops, and ticket machines across Belgium. Decline it every time without exception.
Brussels — Gare du Midi / Brussels-Midi
Brussels-Midi station — the worst neighbourhood in the city
Brussels-Midi is where Eurostar, Thalys, and most international trains arrive. The immediate surroundings — the streets around the station on the south side — are the least safe and least pleasant area of central Brussels. This is relevant in two ways: do not book accommodation near Brussels-Midi because it is “central” (it is central to the rail network, not to the city’s attractions), and do not linger outside the station after dark. The metro is the fastest exit from Midi to anywhere worth being: Line 2/6 toward Arts-Loi, Trone, or Porte de Namur puts you in a completely different Brussels within three stops.
Where to stay insteadIxelles, Saint-Gilles, or the area around Place Sainte-Catherine and the Grand Place (Brussels-Central station, a 10-minute IC ride from Midi or one metro stop). These neighbourhoods give you actual Brussels at prices often lower than Midi-adjacent hotels.
Belgium-wide — SNCB train tickets
Not buying a Rail Pass or Go Pass for multi-city trips
A single adult point-to-point ticket Brussels–Bruges costs €15.90. The Rail Pass (10 journeys, €97) brings each journey to €9.70. A Brussels–Bruges–Ghent–Antwerp–Brussels circuit uses 4 journeys and costs €38.80 with the Pass versus €52–58 in single tickets. If you will take four or more domestic train journeys during your trip, the Rail Pass pays for itself. For under-26 travelers the Go Pass 10 (€57, ten journeys at €5.70 each) is even more compelling. Buy at any staffed SNCB station or at sncb.be before travel begins.
Tourist Traps
5 traps
Brussels
Brussels · all visitors
The Manneken Pis — the most disappointing statue in Europe
The Manneken Pis is a genuine cultural icon and entirely worth seeing. It is also 61 centimetres tall, surrounded by crowds, and set on a street corner between two souvenir shops. Visitors who expect a major monument are uniformly let down. The correct way to experience the Manneken Pis is to walk past it, acknowledge it, and continue without building a significant detour around it. It is a symbol of Brussels’ irreverent self-image — the joke being that the city’s most famous monument is tiny and slightly absurd. Understanding this, the statue is actually delightful. Expecting a landmark, it is a disappointment. Manage expectations accordingly.
Brussels · most neighborhoods
Organised coach tours of Brussels
Brussels is a city of neighbourhoods, and its best qualities — the Horta Art Nouveau houses, the Marolles flea market, the Matongé African quarter, the Ixelles ponds — are discovered on foot through specific districts. Coach tours and hop-on hop-off buses string together the monument facades without giving you any of the city. Brussels rewards self-directed walking far more than any organised tour. Buy a 24h STIB transit pass (€8.50) and use the metro and tram to get between neighbourhoods; walk within each. The tourist office at Grand Place offers free maps and suggested walking routes.
Bruges
Bruges · July and August weekends
Visiting Bruges on a summer weekend without planning
Bruges on a Saturday in July or August is one of the most crowded tourist experiences in northern Europe. Day-tripper buses arrive from Brussels, Paris, Amsterdam, and London simultaneously. The Markt, Burg, and Rozenhoedkaai become impenetrable at peak hours. Hotels charge €250–450 for rooms that cost €120–180 in May. Either visit on a weekday in shoulder season, or stay overnight. The city at 19:00 after the day-trippers leave is a completely different place — quiet canals, empty squares, and the medieval atmosphere that photographs promise. One overnight in Bruges is worth more than two day-trip visits.
The alternativeGhent is equally beautiful, less visited, and 30 minutes from Brussels by IC (32 min, €8.70). It has the same Flemish architecture, a world-class altarpiece, better restaurants, and a fraction of Bruges’ tourist density. Most visitors who go to Ghent prefer it.
Nationwide
Belgium-wide · beer
Ordering non-Belgian beer in Belgium
Belgium produces over 1,500 beer varieties — Trappist ales, Lambics, Saisons, Gueuzes, Quadrupels — representing the most complex beer culture in the world. Ordering a Heineken, Stella Artois (brewed by AB InBev, technically Belgian but industrial), or Corona in a Belgian café is not technically wrong, but it misses the point entirely — and most good Belgian cafés will give you a look. Ask for the beer list and order by style. If uncertain, “un Trappiste” will get you a Chimay, Westmalle, or Orval depending on the house. Cantillon Gueuze if the café stocks it. Anything marked “artisanale” or listing a small Belgian brewery.
Belgium-wide · fries
Belgian fries from a tourist restaurant rather than a friterie
Belgian frites are a national art form: thick-cut, double-fried in beef fat, served in a paper cone with mayonnaise or a choice of sauces. The restaurant version — ordered as a side dish in tourist zones — is rarely the same product. The correct Belgian frite is from a friterie (frituur in Flemish), a stand-alone fries stall. Every Belgian city has several. Order a “cornet” (cone) with andalouse (tomato-mayo), samurai (spicy mayo), or plain mayo. Eat standing up. This costs €3–4 and is one of the genuinely best things you will eat in Belgium. No table, no cutlery, no €12 side dish required.
Classic First-Timer Mistakes
7 mistakes
Culture — the mistake with the most consequences
Speaking the wrong language in the wrong region
Belgium is divided into two major linguistic communities: Dutch-speaking Flanders (north: Bruges, Ghent, Antwerp, Mechelen) and French-speaking Wallonia (south: Namur, Liège, Dinant). Brussels is officially bilingual but predominantly French-speaking. Beginning a conversation in French in a Flemish city is perceived as a cultural slight — the linguistic divide carries historical weight that visitors from outside Belgium typically underestimate. The correct approach in Flemish cities: begin in English (universally spoken), not French. In Wallonia and Brussels: French or English. In Ypres: English is the natural choice given the WWI context. German-speaking communities exist in the east near Spa, but English works everywhere.
The practical solutionLearn three words: “Bonjour” for Brussels and Wallonia, “Goedendag” for Flanders. Open with English everywhere and you will be understood and welcomed. The key is not speaking perfect Dutch or French — it is not defaulting to the wrong language.
Itinerary planning — universally common
Visiting only Brussels and Bruges
Brussels and Bruges are the two cities that most visitors to Belgium know before they arrive. Both are worth visiting. Neither represents Belgium fully. Ghent is equally beautiful to Bruges, far less crowded, and 32 minutes from Brussels. Antwerp is the cultural and fashion capital of Flanders, 35 minutes from Brussels. Liège offers a genuinely different, working-class Walloon character. The Ardennes is a different country entirely from Bruges. Belgium is extremely compact: every city on this list is within 1h 40min of Brussels by IC train. There is no reason to limit a 5-day visit to two cities when five or six are easily accessible.
Museum planning — Bruges and Ghent
Not knowing which museums close on which days
The Groeningemuseum in Bruges is closed on Mondays. Most Bruges city museums are closed Mondays. The Rubens House in Antwerp is closed Mondays. The Magritte Museum in Brussels is closed Mondays. If you are planning a museum-heavy day and it is Monday, you need a different plan. The In Flanders Fields Museum in Ypres is open daily but check hours in off-peak season. Always verify opening days for any specific attraction before building a day around it.
Bruges — accommodation
Not booking Bruges accommodation well in advance
Bruges has a limited supply of hotels within the historic centre. In peak season (April–October), quality accommodation at reasonable prices books out weeks to months ahead. Visitors who search for Bruges hotels two weeks out in summer find either very expensive rooms or accommodation outside the historic area requiring a bus or bike to access the centre. Book Bruges accommodation as soon as your dates are confirmed. Weekday rates are 30–50% lower than weekend rates. If Bruges is full or too expensive, Ghent (30 min by IC, generally lower prices) makes an excellent alternative base for day-tripping into Bruges.
Dining — timing in Flanders vs Wallonia
Treating Belgium as a late-dining country
Belgian dining hours differ significantly from France or Spain. Most Belgian restaurants begin dinner service at 18:30–19:00 and kitchens close by 21:30–22:00, earlier in Flemish cities than in Brussels or Wallonia. Arriving at a Bruges restaurant at 21:00 expecting to eat is likely to result in a kitchen-closed response. The correct approach is to be seated by 19:30 at the latest in Flanders, by 20:00–20:30 in Brussels. Lunch is the meal to prioritise in Belgium — the dagschotel (daily special, typically €12–18 for two courses) is identical food at 40–50% of the dinner price.
Hotel breakfast — Belgium-wide
Paying for hotel breakfast when a bakery is around the corner
Belgian hotel breakfasts cost €15–25 and are rarely worth the price unless included in your rate. A croissant, a pain au chocolat, and a coffee at the bakery around the corner costs €5–7 and is the actual Belgian morning experience. Belgian bakeries (boulangeries in French, bakkerijen in Dutch) open early and produce excellent pastries. The praline chocolate croissant at a proper Flemish bakery is better than anything on a hotel breakfast buffet. If your rate includes breakfast, eat it; if not, opt out and explore the neighbourhood instead.
Sunday planning — Belgium-wide
Arriving in a Belgian city on Sunday without a plan
Belgium takes Sunday seriously. Most shops outside city centres close. Many restaurants close Sunday evening or take Monday instead. Supermarkets in smaller cities often close from noon Sunday. In Bruges and Ghent, Sunday is paradoxically crowded with day-trippers (markets, cafes) but logistically awkward for errands. Plan Sunday specifically: know which markets operate (Ghent’s Sunday flea market at Bevrijdingsplein, Brussels’ Jeu de Balle flea market every morning), which bakeries open, and where dinner will be. Arriving anywhere in the Ardennes on Sunday afternoon without a reservation for dinner is a reliable way to eat from a petrol station.
