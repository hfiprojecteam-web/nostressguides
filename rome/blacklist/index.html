Restaurants to Avoid
6 patterns
Centro Storico
Piazza Navona · Piazza della Rotonda · Piazza di Spagna
Restaurants on the piazzas
Any restaurant with outdoor tables facing Piazza Navona, the Pantheon, or the Spanish Steps is selling the view, not the food. Pasta dishes run €18–25 for quality worth €8–10. The coperto is often inflated to €4–5 per person. The food is reliably forgettable. Walk two minutes down any side street and prices halve with quality intact.
The tellStaff soliciting from the doorway, menus in eight languages with photos, no Romans eating there at any hour.
Via della Conciliazione · Borgo area
Everything near the Vatican entrance
The entire strip leading to St. Peter's is Rome's highest concentration of tourist-trap restaurants. Laminated menus, staff at the door, €18–20 pasta that has never met quality ingredients. Walk 10 minutes into Prati — Via Cola di Rienzo, Via Candia, Via Bettolo — and the same meal costs half as much and actually tastes like Rome.
ExceptionPizzarium Bonci on Via della Meloria is excellent and nearby — but it's pizza al taglio, not a sit-down restaurant.
Trastevere
Piazza di Santa Maria in Trastevere · Piazza Trilussa
Restaurants directly on the main piazzas
The same pattern as Centro Storico applies here. Piazza Santa Maria in Trastevere is beautiful — eat in it for a drink, not for dinner. The restaurants facing it charge 30–40% more than equivalent places two streets away, with noticeably worse food. Walk to Via dei Vascellari, Via della Lungaretta, or the quieter streets south of Viale Trastevere.
Near Monuments
Via Sacra / Colosseum perimeter
Restaurants around the Colosseum
The restaurants immediately around the Colosseum — especially on Via Sacra and Via dei Fori Imperiali — serve tourists who haven't planned lunch and are already tired. Poor quality, high prices, no Roman customers. Walk 10 minutes north to Monti. The same walk that's free gets you to some of Rome's most honest cooking.
Any neighbourhood
"Menù turistico" at fixed price
A fixed-price tourist menu is a reliable signal that a restaurant has optimised for throughput rather than quality. €15–20 buys water, bread, a forgettable primo, and a forgettable secondo. The same €20 at a market stall or a trattoria without the tourist menu will be significantly better. If you see "Tourist Menu" in the window, keep walking.
What to look for insteadHandwritten menu or printed without photos. Tables occupied by Italians. No one asking you to come inside. Reservations required for dinner.
Everywhere
Coffee sitting at a famous piazza
An espresso at the counter of any Roman bar costs €1–1.30. The same espresso sitting at a table on Piazza Navona costs €6–8. The coffee is identical. You are paying for the table rental. This is legal and not a scam — but knowing this in advance helps you decide whether the setting is worth the premium. Sant'Eustachio and Tazza d'Oro near the Pantheon are both genuinely excellent at the counter. At a table, they're just expensive.
Tours Not Worth the Money
5 to skip
City-wide
Hop-on hop-off bus
Rome's historic centre is compact and largely pedestrianised. A hop-on hop-off bus can't enter most of it. The Colosseum, Vatican, Trastevere, and Centro Storico are all reached more effectively on foot, by Metro, or by bus. The HOHO bus costs €25–35 for a circuit that skips the streets where Rome actually happens. The views from an open-top bus on Via dei Fori Imperiali are real — but you can walk that same stretch for free in 20 minutes.
Better optionRoma 24H pass (€7) covers all ATAC transport. Metro + walking covers every major site more efficiently than any bus tour.
Vatican area
"Skip the line" Vatican tours from street vendors
Vendors outside the Vatican Museums offer "skip the line" tours at €70–80 per person. An official timed-entry ticket on museivaticani.va costs €25. The guided tours themselves are legitimate services — what is not legitimate is the implication that you cannot book direct. A €25 timed reservation provides essentially the same entry experience. Only pay for a guided tour if you want a guide, not to avoid a queue.
Active scam versionSome vendors near the entrance sell fake tickets or tickets at massively inflated prices. Only buy from museivaticani.va or an authorised agent.
Colosseum area
Gladiator costume photo on the Colosseum steps
Men in "gladiator" or centurion costumes near the Colosseum offer to pose for photos. No price is quoted until after the photo is taken, at which point demands of €20–50 per photo emerge. These are not performers — they are not authorised, the costumes are cheap, and the confrontation when you don't pay is unpleasant. If you want a photo with the Colosseum, take it yourself from any angle.
How to avoidDon't engage. If approached, keep walking. Once a photo is taken, you are in a difficult position.
City-wide
Third-party resellers for Colosseum and Vatican
Platforms on the first page of Google results often charge €40–60 for Colosseum tickets that cost €18–20 direct, and €40–70 for Vatican tickets that cost €25 direct. They are not adding meaningful value. The official websites — ticketing.colosseo.it and museivaticani.va — are straightforward to use, sell the same tickets, and include no markup. The one legitimate reason to use a third party is for guided tours with an actual guide.
City-wide
Catacombs "combo" tours with multiple sites
Several operators sell catacombs + Appian Way + other sites in a single half-day tour. The catacombs are genuinely interesting and require a guided tour to enter — but the combo format rushes each site and inflates the price. The Catacombs of San Callisto and San Sebastiano on the Appian Way are accessible independently by bus (taking the 118 from Circo Massimo). Walk-up guided tours are available on-site for €8–10.
Tourist Traps
5 to know
Trevi Fountain perimeter
Souvenir shops and restaurants within 200 metres of Trevi
A €0.50 bottle of water costs €3–5 from a vendor near Trevi. Souvenir magnets are €5–8 each. The same magnets near Termini cost €1–2. The restaurants on the streets directly around the fountain follow the monument-proximity pricing pattern — inflated costs, tourist-facing menus, negligible quality. Buy nothing near the fountain except the experience of seeing it.
Nasone tipRome has hundreds of free public drinking fountains (nasoni) — small iron spouts on walls and streets. They run cold, clean water year-round. Carry a bottle and refill it.
Near Vatican
Rosaries and Pope merchandise near St. Peter's
Shops within five minutes of St. Peter's sell rosaries at €15–40 that are identical to those sold elsewhere in Rome for €3–8. Pope-related merchandise — calendars, mugs, portraits — commands a 200–300% premium in the Vatican perimeter. If you want religious items, buy them in a tabacchi or a legitimate religious goods shop further from the basilica. The blessing is the same regardless of where the rosary was purchased.
Centro Storico
San Lorenzo leather market
The leather market near San Lorenzo — and similar "artisan leather" stalls elsewhere in the centre — sells machine-made goods at handcraft prices. Quality is inconsistent, prices are negotiable but start inflated, and the "made in Italy" claims are often misleading for mass-market items. Genuine Florentine leather goods are better bought in Florence. In Rome, the artisan leather workshops in the Ghetto area (Via del Portico d'Ottavia, surrounding streets) are more reliably authentic.
Everywhere
Bottled water from ambulant vendors
The rolling coolers near the Colosseum, Trevi, and Spanish Steps sell water for €3–5 per small bottle. A supermarket — there are several within five minutes of every major attraction — sells the same bottle for €0.30–0.50. Alternatively, Rome's nasoni fountains provide free cold drinking water throughout the city. Refuse vendors, find a supermarket, or use a fountain.
Campo de' Fiori
Market souvenirs at tourist prices
Campo de' Fiori's morning market has a split personality: the produce and flowers are genuine, fresh, and honestly priced; the souvenir section sells the same magnets, ceramics, and "artisan" goods as every other tourist market at inflated prices. Buy fruit, flowers, and cheese here. For souvenirs, the Testaccio market or shops in residential neighbourhoods away from major sights offer better value.
Active Scams 2026
6 active
Roma Termini · Fiumicino Airport · Ciampino
Unofficial taxi drivers inside terminals
Men approach arrivals inside Termini station and both airports offering "taxi" or "private transfer" services. These are not licensed taxis. Fares are invented on the spot — commonly €80–150 for journeys that should cost €14–50. In some cases, luggage is held until payment is made. Official taxis are white, wait at designated stands outside the terminal, and use a meter or fixed rate. Never accept any approach inside.
Fixed ratesFiumicino to central Rome: €50. Ciampino to central Rome: €31. No negotiation, no variation for groups.
Metro Line A · Bus 64 · Colosseum area · Trevi Fountain
Pickpockets on crowded transit and at monuments
Rome's most active pickpocket zones are Metro Line A (especially Termini, Spagna, Ottaviano stops), Bus 64 from Termini to the Vatican, and the areas immediately around the Colosseum and Trevi Fountain. Teams typically work in pairs — one causes a distraction or creates crowding, the other takes the wallet. Phones in back pockets and bags worn on the back are primary targets.
ProtectionFront crossbody bag. Phone in front pocket. Keep bags closed and in front in any crowd. Nothing of value in back pockets.
Trevi Fountain · Spanish Steps · Tourist areas generally
Friendship bracelet / rose seller
A man approaches and ties a bracelet around your wrist "as a gift" or presses flowers into your companion's hands. Once the bracelet is on or flowers accepted, payment is demanded aggressively — typically €10–20. The "gift" was never a gift. Refusing after acceptance is unpleasant and sometimes escalates.
PreventionDon't let anyone touch your hands or wrists without consent. Say "No grazie" firmly and keep walking without slowing down. Don't touch the roses.
Metro stations · Termini
Helpful strangers at ticket machines
Someone offers unsolicited help navigating the ticket machine — typically directing you to buy a more expensive option or suggesting you hand them cash to buy the ticket for you. The help ends with a request for payment. Rome's Metro machines are in English and straightforward — you do not need assistance. Decline any approach that begins with unsolicited offers to help you use a machine.
Restaurants and shops · ATMs
Dynamic Currency Conversion (DCC)
When paying by card, you may be asked: "Do you want to pay in euros or in dollars/pounds?" Choosing your home currency triggers Dynamic Currency Conversion — a markup of 3–7% applied by the terminal, not your bank. This is legal but misleading. Always choose to pay in euros. The same applies at ATMs — always select to be charged in the local currency.
Worst offendersEuronet ATMs (bright blue machines near tourist sites) charge both a €5–7 transaction fee and a poor exchange rate. Use bank-branded ATMs: Intesa Sanpaolo, UniCredit, BNL.
Restaurants — everywhere
Two-menu trick
Some tourist-oriented restaurants keep two menus: a photo menu with higher prices handed to apparent tourists, and a plain text menu for everyone else. Occasionally the prices on the two versions differ by 20–40%. Ask for the menu without photos, or check prices before sitting down. Discrepancies between a menu on the door and the menu at the table are a red flag — leave before ordering.
Classic First-Timer Mistakes
7 mistakes
Planning
Not booking the Colosseum and Vatican in advance
Arriving at the Colosseum without a ticket means joining a queue that can exceed 90 minutes in high season — only to find the day is sold out. The same applies to the Vatican Museums and Galleria Borghese. All three require advance booking. Colosseum tickets open 30 days before your visit. Vatican Museums open 60 days ahead. Galleria Borghese requires a reservation — there is no walk-in option.
Time costArriving without a ticket can cost 1–2 hours of waiting. In a 3-day trip, that is significant.
Transport
Taking the Leonardo Express instead of the FL1
The Leonardo Express (€14) runs non-stop from Fiumicino to Termini. The FL1 regional train (€8) runs the same route with intermediate stops, including Trastevere station. If you're staying in Trastevere, Testaccio, or Ostiense, the FL1 drops you closer to your accommodation and costs €6 less. Many visitors automatically take the more expensive option without knowing the alternative exists.
Transport
Not validating transit tickets
Paper bus and tram tickets must be validated at the yellow machine when you board. Metro tickets validate at the turnstile. An unvalidated ticket is treated the same as no ticket — inspectors check regularly and issue €50+ fines with no exceptions for tourist confusion. Contactless payment validates automatically, but only if you tap the reader.
Sightseeing
Visiting major sites at midday
The Colosseum, Forum, and Palatine Hill have almost no shade. In June through August, midday temperatures exceed 35°C and the stone amplifies the heat. The sites are also most crowded from 10:00–14:00. The 08:30 opening slot is substantially cooler, less crowded, and more enjoyable. The same principle applies to all outdoor sites — any visit between 11:00 and 15:00 in summer involves significant physical discomfort.
Driving
Driving into the ZTL
The ZTL (Zona a Traffico Limitato) covers the historic centre, Trastevere, and other areas. Electronic cameras photograph every entering vehicle. Unauthorized drivers receive fines of €80–100 per infraction, mailed to their home address months after the trip. Many rental car companies add their own handling fee of €30–50 on top. The signs are small and easy to miss. If you have a rental car, do not drive into central Rome under any circumstances.
If you need to reach your hotelCall the hotel before arrival — they can register your plate for a brief drop-off window. Park at Metro termini (Anagnina, Laurentina, Ponte Mammolo) and use public transport.
Dining
Eating dinner at 18:30 or 19:00
Roman restaurants begin dinner service at 19:30–20:00. Arriving at 18:30 means either an empty restaurant that has not yet started service, or a tourist-oriented place that opens early specifically to catch confused visitors. Romans eat dinner at 21:00. Arriving at 20:00–20:30 for a booking puts you in the first proper service. The food from a kitchen that hasn't yet warmed up is often worse than later in the evening.
Jubilee 2026
Not accounting for Jubilee crowd patterns
2026 is a Holy Year. Easter Week (April 1–6) will see the Vatican overwhelmed beyond normal peak conditions — plan around it or accept extraordinary waits. Major feast days cause St. Peter's Square to close for events. Accommodation near the Vatican will run 15–25% above typical rates. None of this makes Rome impossible — it simply requires earlier booking, alternative neighbourhood choices, and slightly more flexibility on Vatican timing.
