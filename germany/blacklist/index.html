Restaurants • Transport • Tourist Traps • Classic Mistakes
Germany has some of Europe’s best food, most reliable transport, and most extraordinary cultural sites. The patterns on this list are not failures of Germany — they are the predictable layer of tourist-facing infrastructure that attaches itself to any country’s most famous addresses. Knowing them means you reach the real thing faster.
How to use this list These are patterns, not verdicts. Individual restaurants vary; not every Hofbräuhaus visitor has a bad meal; not every Rhine cruise is overpriced relative to its context. Use this list as a calibration tool for the moments when something feels wrong, not as a prohibition list.

Restaurant Traps
5 patterns
Munich — tourist beer hall traps
Munich — Hofbräuhaus am Platzl
The Hofbräuhaus: a beer hall for tourists, not for Munich
The Hofbräuhaus is one of the most famous buildings in Bavaria and is worth visiting once to understand what it is. What it is: a 5,000-seat tourist processing facility serving €14.50 Maß (1-litre beer), unremarkable German food at elevated prices, to an audience that is 95% international tourists and no Munich residents whatsoever. Munich’s residents drink at the Augustiner-Keller, Hofbräukeller (different institution), Paulaner Bräuhaus, or the English Garden beer garden by the Chinese Tower. The Hofbräuhaus experience is entirely legitimate as long as it is clearly understood as what it is, at the price it charges.
The real alternative Augustiner-Keller (Arnulfstraße 52) is Munich’s most traditional beer garden — self-service, local prices (Maß €9.50–10.50), and filled with actual Munich residents on warm evenings. Bring cash.
Munich — Marienplatz tourist restaurant ring
Restaurants facing Marienplatz: tourist prices, tourist quality
The square-facing restaurants on and immediately around Munich’s Marienplatz charge 35–50% above the Munich average for the same dishes: Weißwurst €9–12 (vs €4.50–6 at a Metzgerei), Schweinebraten €22–28 (vs €14–18 two streets back). Viktualienmarkt, 200 metres from Marienplatz, has prepared food stalls, a central beer garden, and local delicatessens at genuine Munich prices. Eating at the Viktualienmarkt instead of the Marienplatz restaurants is the single most effective food decision you can make in central Munich.
Correct move Walk south from Marienplatz to Viktualienmarkt (3 minutes). Grab a Weißwurst and Brezn at a stand, then take a beer from the central beer garden. Cash preferred.
Berlin — Alexanderplatz and TV Tower restaurant zone
Restaurants around Alexanderplatz: worst value in Berlin
The area around Alexanderplatz and the base of the Berlin TV Tower (Fernsehturm) has the highest concentration of tourist-facing, low-quality, high-price restaurants in the city. The TV Tower revolving restaurant (Sphere) charges €35–50+ per person for average food in a spectacular position — the view from the tower observation deck (€22.50, no reservation, incredible) is far better value than the restaurant. Street-level restaurants on Karl-Liebknecht-Straße and around the World Clock are overpriced for everything from currywurst to schnitzel.
Where to eat in East Berlin Prenzlauer Berg (10 min by U-Bahn from Alexanderplatz) has Berlin’s best neighbourhood dining. Kastanienallee and Kollwitzplatz have excellent restaurants at honest prices with almost no tourists.
Cologne — Dom-facing Brauhaus imitations
Cologne: tourist Brauhaus vs. genuine Kölsch institution
Kölsch beer is uniquely tied to Cologne — it can only legally be brewed in the Cologne area under the Kölsch Convention. The tradition is served in small 200ml Stangen glasses, automatically refilled until you say Nein danke. Brauhaus restaurants facing the cathedral on Domkloster and Am Dom are tourist venues charging €3.50–4 for a Stange of Kölsch (vs €2–2.50 in a genuine Brauhaus). The two authentic Kölsch institutions worth seeking out are Früh am Dom (the oldest surviving brewery/Brauhaus in central Cologne) and Haus Töller in the Belgisches Viertel for a local neighbourhood context.
Genuine Kölsch Früh am Dom (Am Hof 12, directly behind the Dom) is technically in a tourist location but operates as a genuine Brauhaus with local prices. Gaffel am Dom is similar. Avoid the table-service restaurants on the Dom forecourt itself.
Germany-wide — tourist-trap currywurst
Currywurst: €8–12 at tourist stands vs. €3.50 at local Imbiss
Currywurst (sliced pork sausage with curry ketchup) is Berlin’s defining street food and costs €3–4 at a genuine Berlin Imbiss (snack stand). Tourist-positioned stands around Checkpoint Charlie, the Brandenburg Gate, and the Reichstag charge €8–12 for the same product — often with a branded packaging upgrade and no improvement in taste whatsoever. Curry 36 (Mehringdamm 36, Kreuzberg), Konopke’s Imbiß (Schönhauser Allee, Prenzlauer Berg), and Konnopke’s in the elevated U-Bahn arch are the benchmark currywurst addresses in Berlin at local prices.
Price benchmark A currywurst mit Pommes (with fries) should cost €4.50–6 at a genuine Imbiss. Over €7 for a basic currywurst without significant provenance is a tourist surcharge. Pay cash.
Transport Traps
5 patterns
Germany-wide — DB ticket machines
Buying the wrong ticket type: Flex vs. Sparpreis
DB ticket machines at German stations default to showing the full-price flexible (Flexpreis) ticket rather than the discounted Sparpreis advance fare. Buying a Hamburg–Berlin ticket at the station machine on the day can cost €59–79 (Flexpreis) when the same journey bought 3 days ahead online costs €17.90 (Sparpreis). The machine’s default screen does not prominently signal that you are buying the most expensive ticket type. Always check bahn.de or the DB Navigator app before arriving at a machine — know your target price before you start.
Correct approach Book Sparpreis tickets via the DB Navigator app or bahn.de at least 3 days ahead. At the machine, look for the “Sparpreis” or “Angebote” (offers) option before confirming.
Germany-wide — Schwarzfahren risk
Travelling without a valid ticket: €60 minimum fine
German urban transit (U-Bahn, S-Bahn, trams, buses) operates on an honour system — there are no barriers, and inspectors (Kontrolleure) board randomly in plain clothes. The fine for travelling without a valid ticket (Schwarzfahren) is €60, payable immediately. Inspectors are not flexible and the fine cannot be waived by explaining you did not know. Validate your ticket (stamp it in the machine on the platform or at the bus door) before boarding. This applies even to Deutschlandticket holders — carry the digital or physical ticket and be ready to show it. Tourist groups consistently underestimate enforcement frequency.
Important note On Munich and Hamburg S-Bahn and U-Bahn: tap the yellow/orange validation machine with your ticket before boarding. An unvalidated ticket is not a valid ticket regardless of whether you paid for it.
Airports — unlicensed taxi touts and currency exchange
Airport arrivals: two consistent overcharge points
At Frankfurt, Munich, and Berlin Brandenburg airports, two consistent overcharge patterns apply: (1) Unlicensed taxi drivers inside the arrivals hall quoting fixed prices 40–80% above the metered taxi fare. Use only metered taxis from the official rank outside arrivals, or take the airport S-Bahn/U-Bahn (S-Bahn from Frankfurt Airport to city: €5.60; Munich Airport S-Bahn to Hauptbahnhof: €13.60 or covered by CityTourCard). (2) Currency exchange counters in arrivals halls offering “no commission” exchange at rates 8–15% worse than your bank card’s interbank rate. Use your card everywhere. Withdraw euros from an ATM if cash is needed.
What to do Take the airport express train. Use your bank card for all purchases. If you need cash, use a bank-owned ATM (Sparkasse, Deutsche Bank, Commerzbank), not the currency exchange counter.
Germany-wide — Dynamic Currency Conversion
DCC: paying in your home currency costs 3–8% extra
When paying by card in Germany, terminal screens frequently offer to charge in your home currency (USD, GBP, AUD, etc.) rather than euros. This is Dynamic Currency Conversion — applied at rates 3–8% worse than your card’s interbank rate, with the difference kept by the merchant. Always select “In EUR bezahlen” (Pay in EUR) or “Weiter in EUR” (Continue in EUR) when prompted. The prompt is often displayed in German — look for “EUR” as one of the options and select it before confirming.
Key German phrase “Ich möchte in Euro bezahlen.” (I would like to pay in euros.) Say this before the terminal is turned toward you in tourist-area restaurants and shops.
Bavaria — Neuschwanstein shuttle bus
Private shuttle touts vs. official Alpseestraße bus
From Hohenschwangau village to Neuschwanstein, three options exist: walk 40 minutes uphill, take the official horse-drawn carriage (€8 up / €4 down), or take the municipal bus (€3 up / €2 down). Private shuttle operators at the Ticket Centre parking lot offer “direct” transport at €12–20 per person and imply it is the only or fastest option. The official horse-drawn carriages and the marked walking path both reach the castle gate — the bus stop is clearly marked at the Ticket Centre. Take the official Alpseestraße bus (runs every 20 min in season) or walk; do not pay €20 to a private shuttle for a 7-minute ride that costs €3 on the official service.
Official transport Municipal bus stop is at the Ticket Centre, marked “Alpseestraße”. Horse-drawn carriages depart from the same area. Buy a ticket at the horse carriage booth or pay cash to the bus driver. The walk via the forest path is scenic and takes 35–40 minutes.
Tourist Traps
5 patterns
Berlin — Checkpoint Charlie
Checkpoint Charlie: the site is significant, the experience is not
Checkpoint Charlie was the most famous crossing point between East and West Berlin during the Cold War. The site is historically important. What is there now: a replica guardhouse in the middle of a busy intersection, actors in US Army uniforms charging €5–10 for a photograph, and a souvenir shop selling faux Soviet memorabilia. The Checkpoint Charlie Museum (€16.50) beside it is significantly better and worth visiting for the documented escape stories. The East Side Gallery (free, 20 minutes by S-Bahn) conveys the historical weight of the Wall more powerfully than the Checkpoint Charlie site. Visit briefly, don’t pay the photograph actors.
Better alternative The Berlin Wall Memorial on Bernauer Straße (free, outdoor exhibition) shows a preserved section of the death strip with full historical context. Better documentation, free, not commercialised.
Rhine Valley — day cruise packages from Cologne
Full-day Rhine cruise packages: slow, expensive, and misses the best section
Several operators sell packaged full-day Rhine cruises from Cologne (€40–60 per person) covering the full river from Cologne to Koblenz. The best section of the Rhine Gorge (Loreley, Bacharach, Burg Katz, Burg Maus) is between Boppard and Rüdesheim — south of Koblenz. A full-day cruise from Cologne to Koblenz misses this section entirely. The correct approach: take the IC train from Cologne to Koblenz (1h) or Bacharach (1h 20m), then take the KD Line cruise on the Loreley section specifically (1h 30m), then train back. Less expensive, focused on the best section, no dead-time on flat river.
Correct approach KD Rhine Line from Bacharach to St Goarshausen (Loreley section, ~1h 15m southbound) is €18–24. Buy at the KD kiosk on the river bank in Bacharach. Book the morning train from Cologne to Bacharach on the DB app the night before.
Berlin — Reichstag dome “tour” resellers
Third-party Reichstag dome booking services charging for a free experience
The Reichstag dome is free to visit — registration at bundestag.de is required and costs nothing. Third-party websites rank prominently in search results for “Reichstag dome ticket” and charge €8–25 for what is legally free. They are providing a legitimate (if overpriced) booking service, but the service exists only because the official registration system requires direct sign-up at bundestag.de — which is not complicated. Book directly. The only legitimate Reichstag dome access is through bundestag.de. Any other site charging money for dome access is adding a margin for no added value.
Correct booking Go to bundestag.de → “Visitor Service” → “Registration for visiting the dome.” Fill in names and dates. Free. Confirmation by email. Bring the confirmation and a photo ID to the entrance.
Munich — Oktoberfest tent “reservation” services
Third-party Oktoberfest tent reservations: not what they appear
Tent reservations for Oktoberfest are legally allocated only through the official Munich city lottery and via direct brewery relationships — the allocation is completed by January–February each year. Third-party companies selling “guaranteed” Oktoberfest tent reservations at €40–80 per person are typically selling pre-purchased table reservations that require a minimum food/drink spend of €50–100 per person on top of the reservation fee. Alternatively, the tents are freely accessible without reservation until the pre-reserved sections fill up — arriving before 11:00 on weekdays (not Saturdays, not the opening weekend) gives reasonable access to unreserved standing areas at the bar without paying any reservation surcharge.
Without a reservation Weekday mornings before noon: queue for a seat at an unreserved table. Bring cash for Maß and pretzels. Do not arrive on the first weekend or any Saturday expecting to get in without a reservation.
Füssen — Neuschwanstein without the Marienbrücke detour
Visiting Neuschwanstein without walking to the Marienbrücke
The photograph of Neuschwanstein that appears in every travel article — the castle viewed from above, framed by Alpine forest against a mountain backdrop — is taken from the Marienbrücke, a footbridge 150 metres above the Pöllat Gorge. The Marienbrücke is a 20-minute walk from the castle entrance, is free, and is not signposted at the castle itself. A significant proportion of visitors complete the castle interior tour and leave without ever reaching the viewpoint that justifies the visit photographically. Ask at the Ticket Centre for directions, allow 45 extra minutes, and wear shoes suitable for the walk down through the gorge.
Don’t miss it From the castle courtyard, walk past the east wing and continue uphill on the marked path (10–15 min). Marienbrücke offers the classic view. The bridge may close in winter or icy conditions — check on the day.
Classic Mistakes
7 patterns
Germany-wide — Sunday planning
Not planning for Sunday closures: the most consistent planning mistake
Germany has some of Europe’s strictest Sunday trading laws. Virtually all shops, supermarkets, pharmacies, and many services are closed on Sundays in most German cities. Museums (usually open). Restaurants and cafés (usually open). Everything else — including the supermarket where you planned to buy lunch supplies, the pharmacy where you need cold medicine, and the clothing shop where you planned to replace your rained-on jacket — is closed. Plan your Sunday grocery shopping on Saturday. Know where the nearest Sunday-open bakery (Bäckerei) is, as these typically open for morning hours.
Germany-wide — cash expectations
Assuming Germany is card-friendly everywhere
Germany remains significantly more cash-dependent than most Western European countries. Many restaurants, beer gardens, market stalls, small shops, Imbiss stands, and some museums still accept cash only — or add a surcharge for card payment under a certain amount. This is particularly true in Bavaria, at Christmas markets, and at any outdoor venue. Arriving with zero cash and relying entirely on card will result in missed meals and awkward moments. Withdraw €100–150 from a bank-owned ATM upon arrival and treat cash as the primary payment method at smaller or traditional venues.
Germany-wide — Pergamon Museum
Planning a Berlin trip around the Pergamon Altar and arriving to find it closed
The Pergamon Altar — the museum’s centrepiece, a 2nd-century BC Greek temple structure — has been under renovation since 2023 and will not reopen until at least 2027. The Pergamon Museum remains open and is worth visiting for the Ishtar Gate, the Market Gate of Miletus, and the Islamic Art wing. But visitors who travel specifically to see the Altar and do not check ahead will be disappointed. Verify the current open galleries at smb.museum/en/museums-institutions/pergamonmuseum before planning a Berlin trip around this.
Germany-wide — museum closure days
Visiting museums without checking their specific closing day
German museums close on different days depending on operator and federal state. National museums in Berlin close on Mondays. Bavarian state museums (Deutsches Museum, Alte Pinakothek) close on Mondays. The Städel in Frankfurt closes on Mondays. Some Hamburg museums close on Tuesdays. Several small Cologne and Rhine Valley museums are closed Monday and Tuesday. Planning a major museum visit on a Monday without checking will result in a locked door at 9 out of 10 venues. Check the specific museum’s website 48 hours before. Do not rely on travel aggregators for closure information.
Munich — Oktoberfest period
Booking Munich accommodation at normal prices during Oktoberfest without checking dates
Oktoberfest 2026 runs from 19 September to 4 October. During this 16-day period, Munich hotels charge 3–5 times normal rates and book out completely months in advance. Visitors who book Munich for late September without registering that Oktoberfest is running will arrive to find their budget hotel has tripled in price from their booking date (or that their booking confirmation was made before the hotel updated its rates, which occasionally leads to disputes). Check your Munich dates against the Oktoberfest calendar at oktoberfest.de before finalising travel plans.
Germany-wide — tipping expectations
Tipping nothing, or tipping 20%: both are wrong
German tipping culture is different from both American (mandatory 18–22%) and British/French (optional small percentage) norms. In Germany, rounding up the bill or adding 5–10% for good service is the expected norm. A €23.40 bill is typically settled as €25. Tipping nothing for adequate sit-down restaurant service is considered rude by German standards; tipping 20% is unnecessary and will confuse the server. Cash tips are strongly preferred — saying “stimmt so” (that’s correct/keep the change) when paying is the standard German way to signal a tip.
Germany-wide — itinerary scope
Treating “Germany” as just Berlin and Munich
Berlin and Munich are Germany’s two most visited cities and are both genuinely extraordinary. But the parts of Germany most undervisited relative to their quality include: Hamburg (one of Europe’s great port cities, with the Elbphilharmonie and the Speicherstadt); the Rhine Valley (Bacharach, Koblenz, the Loreley section); Dresden (baroque masterpiece rebuilt after 1945); and the medieval towns of the Romantic Road (Rothenburg ob der Tauber, Würzburg). Germany’s ICE rail network makes a multi-city itinerary not only possible but practical in a way that no other country in Europe matches.
